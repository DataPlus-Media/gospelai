For GospelAI, I would recommend creating a feature called Author Insights, allowing users to read what respected Christian theologians and Bible commentators have written about specific Bible verses.

The concept would be similar to the Compare Versions feature in the YouVersion Bible App, except instead of comparing Bible translations, users would compare different authors’ interpretations of the same Scripture.

This could become one of GospelAI’s most valuable features because it would combine traditional biblical scholarship with modern AI-assisted Bible study.

2. Where to get the author commentaries

The good news is that you don’t have to write thousands of commentaries yourself. Several historical works are already digitised.

Source	What it provides
Christian Classics Ethereal Library	Matthew Henry’s commentary, available in multiple downloadable formats
CrossWire SWORD	Downloadable Bible commentary modules and other biblical study resources
STEP Bible	Scripture study resources, including Matthew Henry’s commentary

CCEL also documents ways to retrieve sections of works programmatically.

I would start with Matthew Henry, John Gill, John Calvin and Charles Spurgeon, subject to obtaining suitable digital editions and verifying their reuse rights.

Important: Historical works may be public domain, but modern translations, edited editions, and digital collections can carry separate rights. CrossWire explicitly warns that some of its modules require additional permission for redistribution.

3. How to connect the commentaries to Bible verses

This is the most important technical part.

I would not recommend asking AI to generate an author’s interpretation every time someone selects a verse. Instead, create a searchable commentary database that stores the original writings and links them to the corresponding Scripture.

One complication: commentators don’t always write about individual verses. Matthew Henry might explain John 3:14–21 as a single passage.

Your system should therefore support verse ranges, so the commentary covering John 3:14–21 can appear when a user selects John 3:16.

Example database structure

Field	Example
author	Matthew Henry
book	John
chapter	3
start_verse	14
end_verse	21
commentary_text	Original commentary
source_url	Link to source work
license	Verified reuse permission

A PostgreSQL or Supabase database would work well for this.

4. Where AI could make GospelAI different

Once you’ve stored the original commentary, GPT could provide three optional features.

* Simplify: Rewrite difficult 17th-century English in modern language.
* Compare authors: Explain where Matthew Henry and John Gill agree or disagree.
* Ask questions: Allow users to ask follow-up questions based specifically on the retrieved commentary.

For accuracy, every AI explanation should reference the original passage. Distinguish direct quotations from AI summaries, and don’t attribute ideas to an author unless the source supports them.

This could also reduce API costs: retrieving a stored commentary requires no AI generation. Only explanations and comparisons need an AI call.

5. My recommended development plan

Phase	Work	Priority
1	Obtain Matthew Henry’s commentary and verify rights	High
2	Import and index the commentary by verse or verse range	High
3	Add an Author Insights tab to GospelAI	High
4	Add John Gill and other authors	Medium
5	Introduce AI summaries and comparisons	Medium
6	Add author filters, bookmarks and saved insights	Later

For the first release, I’d focus on original commentary retrieval rather than AI-generated interpretations. That makes the feature cheaper to operate, easier to verify, and more trustworthy.
